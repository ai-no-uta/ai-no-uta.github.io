Evidentials

Introduction

In linguistics, evidentials are grammaticalized morphemes that convey how/whence a proposition is sourced. Is it hearsay? Is it an inference drawn from visual data or auditory data? How much conviction is behind the proposition? I've read vaguely that evidentials display some characteristic properties, like obligatory wide scope: they can't be semantically embedded inside modals, negation, etc. But enough about theory. The purpose of this article is to list some of the common evidentials in Japanese main clauses, describe their usages separately, and make choice comparisons between them when I find that to be helpful. There's a lot of resources on the discussing Japanese evidentials already, but I am writing this nonetheless because 1) it is important to consolidate the discussion in one place and 2) I think I have some novel insights.

みたい

Dictionary Form Present Past
Verbs 引っ越す 引っ越すみたい 引っ越したみたい
い-Adjectives 寒い 寒いみたい 寒かったみたい
な-Adectives 便利 便利みたい 便利だったみたい
Nouns 教師 教師みたい 教師だったみたい
みたい may be followed by the copula だ and can be used attributively, whereupon it is followed by な, as if it were a な-adjective.

To contextualize, let us go over the development of みたい. This morpheme is related to the verb 見る 'see, look'. According to Wiktionary, みたい is derived from 見-た よう see-PST you. You would probably infer that みたい has to do with what you see. This is sometimes true. Let's look at some non-evidential use cases of みたい.

'looks like'. みたい compares a topical object to a standard of comparison.

絵みたいな景色

'a scene that looks like a painting'

'seems like'. みたい constructs a comparison between a topical object and a standard based off any information source.

馬鹿みたいにどもっている。

'stuttering like a fool.'

The use of みたい has been ported over to non-visual comparisons, and this derived sense is visible (ha-ha) in its function as an evidential. Namely, みたい is used to mark inferences that are drawn from first-hand evidence. This information is not necessarily visual.

Context: the air feels damp and you can hear the wind howling outside.

雨が降るみたいだな。

'It seems like it's going to rain.''

Related to みたい is よう, which appears in みたい's etymology as mentioned above. In modern usage, よう functions as みたい's formal counterpart in all senses.

そう1

There are two different morphemes that are both pronounced そう, which is why I am tacking on subscripts. They attach to other words differently and mean different things.

Dictionary Form Present Past
Verbs 引っ越す 引っ越すそう 引っ越したそう
い-Adjectives 寒い 寒いみたい 寒かったそう
な-Adectives 便利 便利だそう 便利だったそう
Nouns 教師 教師だそう 教師だったそう
In short, you can put any plain predicative form in front of そう1. そう1 can be followed by the copula だ and cannot be used attributively.

そう1 conveys hearsay faithfully. You are just repeating back what someone else said. You aren't drawing any novel conclusions of your own, no matter how obvious they might seem.

Context: Someone tells you the sun is rising.

太陽が昇ったそうだ。

'The sun rose.'

そう2

Dictionary Form Present Past
Verbs 引っ越す 引っ越しそう 引っ越しそうだった
い-Adjectives 寒い 寒そう 寒そうだった
な-Adectives 便利 便利なそう 便利なそうだった
Nouns 教師 教師なそう 教師なそうだった
そう2 can be followed by the copula だ. Note from the above that そう2itself receives tense, not the word it is attaching to. This is quite different from the two previous morphemes. Also, when following 良い and ない, the two constructions surface as よさそう and なさそう, respectively. そう2 can be used attributively.

そう2 relays an inference drawn directly from visual information.

Context: there's a tree leaning precariously.

あの木は倒れそうだよ。

'That tree looks like it's going to fall.''

Context: someone placed a slice of cake in front of you. It looks good: the sponge looks moist but light and fluffy, there's ample fresh fruit arranged neatly in the icing layer, and the icing is artfully piped on the top.

ケーキはおいしそう！

'The cake looks tasty!''

おいしそうなケーキ

'a delicious looking cake'

らしい

らしい attaches to words just like みたい, but らしい functions like an い-adjective.

らしい can be used to make comparisons. らしい is used when the topical object is an exemplar of the standard.

Context: Tanaka is Japanese.

田中さんは日本人らしくお辞儀をしました。

Tanaka bowed like a Japanese person.

＃田中さんは日本人みたいにお辞儀をしました

The second sentence with みたい does not suit this context, as Tanaka is a Japanese person; the topical object is not distinct from the standard.

As an evidential, らしい is used to mark an inference based off hearsay.

Context: A coworker tells you that Tanaka has removed the bookmarks to Linkedin and job application portals from his browser. Your coworker says that he seems less agitated, although the boss seems more so and keeps on dragging him to her office.

田中さんは新しい仕事を見つけたらしい。

'It seems that Tanaka has found a new job.'

みたい vs らしい

A comparison (ha-ha) can be drawn for both their non-evidential and evidential senses. When used to make comparisons, みたい is preferred when the topical object is not an example of the exemplar, whereas らしい is the inverse. Hence the following two examples:

Context: Tanaka is Japanese.

田中さんは日本人らしくお辞儀をしました。

#田中さんは日本人みたいにお辞儀をしました。

Context: Billy is not Japanese.

#ビリーさんは日本人らしくお辞儀をしました。

ビリーさんは日本人みたいにお辞儀をしました。

As for their evidential usages, みたい is preferred when the data is obtained firsthand, while らしい is preferred when it is hearsay.

Context: You see and hear a bunch of kids being rowdy screaming at a restaurant.

みんなはうれしいみたいだな。

#みんなはうれしいらしいな。

Context: your friend calls you, and she keeps on pausing to remind others to be quiet.

#みんなはうれしいみたいだな。

みんなはうれしいらしいな。

みたい vs そう2

For そう2, the fact that the evidence is visual is of the utmost importance. For みたい, this is not the case.

Context: Tanaka has a U-haul in front of his house and is loading boxes into it.

#田中さんは引っ越しそうだ。

田中さんは引っ越しするみたいだ。

そう2 is inappropriate here, as the visual nature of the evidence is not important to the inference. You might as well have heard a description of the facts.

Someone cuts open a cake. The sponge looks light and fluffy, there is ample fruit between the icing, the icing on top is piped very elegantly, the sponge is an appetizing colour.

このケーキはおいしそうだ！

#このケーキはおいいみたいだ。

Sorry, giving the context in writing devalues it a little. In this scenario, what is important is the appearance of the cake. I can give my best effort in describing the cake in writing, but the reader still has to form a mental image and use *that* to draw the inference.

そう1 vs everything else

そう1 is the only morpheme listed that does not mark an inference. It is purely faithful to the given information.
