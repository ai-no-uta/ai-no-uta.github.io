You have a name. Your parents have names. Your friends have names. If you know a Chinese person, chances are that you've noticed that they have a name, too. You would be correct in noticing that fact. Chinese people do have names.

Well that's kind of mind numbingly obvious. You might have noticed that Chinese people have given names and surnames. Well, that too is mind numbingly - Hah! Sike! Now this one, this one is definitely not universal amongst humans. Plenty of groups around the world don't have surnames (at least traditionally. Certain governments might force people to adopt surnames) and you do not have to stray very far from China to find them. Just across the border in Myanmar, the dominant Bamar ethnic group doesn't use surnames. Take perhaps the most famous Bamar person in the English speaking world, Aung San Suu Kyi. She has three names:
- Aung San (named after her dad)
- Suu (after her dad's mother)
- Kyi (after her mother)

Three names, zero of which is a surname.

Actually, you don't have to stray from China at all. Ethnic Mongolians (of whom a couple million live within the People's Republic of China's borders) don't have surnames, either.

Okay, so having a surname isn't something you can assume of a person, great. But it also isn't terribly rare, either. So what makes a Chinese surname interesting?

Here is a difference from the English convention: Notice that I have been using the terms surname and given name, rather than last name and first name. This is intentional, because in Chinese, the order is actually swapped! The surname comes first, and it is followed by the given name.

This order is shared with the other countries in Eastern(ish) Asia that have historically been under a lot of Chinese cultural influence: Korea, Japan, Vietnam.

Korea: Kim Jong Un? Surname, given name. 

Japan: Tojo Hideki? Surname, given name. Wait, isn't he called Hideki Tojo? Yes, in western publications. For some reason, while Japanese names are given surname-first in Japanese, the order is swapped in English language publications.

Vietnam: Nguyễn Văn Thiệu? Surname, middle name, given name. (Middle name? Wait what do you mean? Can you talk a little-)

Lets go through the surname and the given name in a little more detail.

The surname.

The modern Chinese word for surname is 姓. It can comprise up to two syllables, although the vast majority of them are monosyllabic. If you've been around Chinese people, you might have noticed that there's not a whole lot of diversity amongst Chinese surnames. You see a lot of Wong/Huang (黃), a lot of Chen/Chan/Tan (陳), a lot of Lin/Lim/Lam (林), for instance. You might not even see much of anything else.

This observation generalizes - the distribution of Chinese surnames is very uneven. The most common surname Wang/(also Wong)/王 has over 100 million holders in mainland China, but by the time you make your way down to the 20th most common, that number has dwindled to a 'measly' 14 million for 羅 ('measly' - hah! That's still more people than in all of say, Sweden). The top 100 out of the 6150 surnames (less than 2%) in use in mainland China accounts for 85% of the population.

You might be surprised to know that 黃、陳、林 do not number among the top 3 most common surnames in China (ranked 7, 5, and 18, respectively). How can this be??? The reason is that some Chinese surnames tend to be regional. 黃、陳、林 predominate in the provinces of Fujian and Guangdong, which are overwhelmingly the origin for the Chinese diaspora in most places around the world, including Southeast Asia, Australia, and the Americas.

After 王 comes Lee/Li/李 and Zhang/Chang/Cheung/張, with each having around 100 million people. 

These two surnames are perceived to be so ubiquitous that they even feature in a Chinese phrase: 張三李四 literally 'Zhang Three Li Four', which is sort of the equivalent to the American English 'Everyday Joe'.

Another common way to refer to the everyman in Chinese is with the phrase 老百姓, or 'old 100 surnames.' Variations of this phrase go back at least 2000 years (see the 商君書)! This reflects both the importance of surnames in Chinese culture and their extremely skewed distribution.

Like in English, Chinese surnames are usually passed from father to child. Unlike in English, a wife usually does *not* take on her husband's surname. Some 20th century women were sometimes referred to using their husbands' surname in English, such as 宋美齡 (Soong Mei-ling) being called Madame Chiang (after her husband 蔣介石 (Chiang Kai-shek)), but this is not conventional in Chinese.


In mainland China and in Taiwan, the surname and the given name are the only names that appear on one's national registration card. But that doesn't mean that these are the only names a Chinese person would have, whether historically or currently.

Kinds of surnames.

Another Chinese word for surname is the compound 姓氏, the first member of which you have already seen. 氏 is yet another word for surname that, when you go back 2300 years, actually meant something different.
