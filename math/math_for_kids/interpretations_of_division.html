Note: I think a big sin when teaching children is that of overexplaining. Children have short attention spans. Trying to wrangle a child into paying attention to the words coming out of your mouth is like trying to wrangle a cat into the bed you just bought specifically for it. It will simply not work. I don't think it's because they don't have the necessary brainpower to understand the concept fully; it's because they lack the concentration to do that. This article, then, isn't meant to present actionable pedagogical ideas; it's just a mental exercise.

Interpretations of division.

There are two ways to think about division.

  1. Quotitive Division. The divisor represents the number of items in each group, and the quotient represents the number of equal groups formed.
  2. Partitive Division. The divisor represents the number of equal groups formed, and the quotient represents the number of items in each.
Both of these interpretations rear their head in elementary school word problems. I present two examples, both to demonstrate this fact (ha!) and also to help those tease apart the definitions who are having trouble with them.
  1. Polly manages an apple orchard that sells 5-apple bags. The day's harvest amounted to 185 apples - how many bags will she need to prepare?
  2. Patricia and her three friends ordered 20 apples from Polly's apple orchard. How many should each friend receive?
Clearly, both interpretations have their uses, depending on the assumed priors of a scenario. It shouldn't be too hard to show their equivalence mathematically; however, I'm not sure if the average elementary schooler will be able to appreciate this much, and they might just become numb to the conceptual difference through exposure.

Before starting this article, I was convinced that the quotitive definition is the definitively mathematically superior choice, but after working out some explanations, my convictions of this have abated somewhat. The partitive definition can be wrangled to fit the scenarios I have imagined.

Dividing 0 by 0

Under the quotitive interpretation, dividing \(0\) by \(0\) is clearly indeterminate. Divide \(0\) things into groups of \(0\).

Q: How many groups can you make?

A: You can make \(0\) groups, or \(1\), or \(2\), or, frankly, any whole number (or fractional parts) of groups. So the answer to the division problem seems to have an even chance of being any number... Because we can't determine the answer, we say this expression is indeterminate.

Under the partitive interpretation, the answer becomes muddled. Divide \(0\) such that there are 0 groups.

Q: How many should each group contain?

A: We don't have any groups, so the amount in each group is a purely hypothetical notion, and thus can be any number.

Actually, this explanation is not bad at all, but it took me an uncomfortable amount of time to think up. I suspect that children might not be willing to think about hypothetical groups.

Dividing other numbers by 0

Under the quotitive interpretation, this clearly diverges. Say we are dividing \(3\) into groups of \(0\). No matter how many groups you have, you won't be able to dent the \(3\) we started with; in some sense, no number is big enough to be the answer.

Under the partitive interpretation, we divide \(3\) into \(0\) groups. A single group can have as many things as it wants. It is hypothetical, so it will never affect the initial \(3\).

Again, the hypothetical reasoning has a potential to cause difficulty, and moreover, I find it difficult to understand why inflating the size of a hypothetical entity would `move toward the right answer'. In the quotitive case, adding groups of 0 at least seems to `move in the right direction', because we are at least adding real things.

Long division.

Under the quotitive interpretation, the traditional wording of division `how many times does the divisor fit into the dividend?' makes sense - the divisor stands for a number of objects in the same vein that the divisor does, so it makes sense that we would try to break the dividend up into groups of the divisor.

Under the partitive interpretation, this needs substantial rewording: we need to find how large each group is; each time the divisor 'fits into the dividend' correspondences to one more object being added to each group. You have to consider all of the groups as changing simultaneously, which is quite taxing.

Dividing by unit fractions

Under the quotitive interpretation, dividing e.g. \(7\) by \(\frac{1}{4}\) involves finding the number of times \(\frac{1}{4}\) fits into \(7\). Since \(\frac{1}{4}\) fits into an individual whole \(4\) times, we also instantly recover the formula \(7*4\).

Under the partitive one, this division is... less bad than I initially thought. It needs just a bit of rewording - rewording that you will see often once you start working with fractions: Given that 7 is \(\frac{1}{4}\) of a whole, how big is a whole? Well, the whole should simply be \(7*4\).
